Route glossary — what each material path represents
R1PRD MRB → Inventory
Material pulled from line side into the PRD MRB (Material Review Board) hold area for review. Once cleared, it is transported back into the inventory store — an added handling loop each time suspect stock is quarantined and released.
R2Receiving → QC → Rework → QC → Inventory
The exception path for inspected material that fails first-pass QC. Rejected items move to the rework rack, where the engineering support team investigates and reworks them, then returns them to QC for re-inspection. Only on approval do they proceed to inventory — the longest and most labor-intensive route.
R3Receiving → Inventory
The standard, highest-volume path. Most received material completes the routine receiving process and moves directly into the inventory store with no inspection detour.
R4Receiving → QC → Inventory
Roughly 20% of received material is sampled for QC inspection. Items that pass on the first attempt — requiring no rework — flow straight from QC into inventory.

How the batch works. Instead of topping up bins one at a time, a whole rack is exchanged in a single swap. Each rack is assumed to hold 70 bins. Refilling one bin in the inventory area takes 92 s, so the 70 bins account for 70 × 92 = 6,440 s. To that we add one 80 s trip — the average round-trip walk to and from the inventory location, incurred just once per rack — and one 15 s rack swap, also once per rack. Together that is 6,535 s per rack, or 93.36 s per bin, against 115.2 s per bin today. About 5% of bins are expected to come back as non-conformances (NCs) — bins that fall outside the rack swap and must be replenished on their own. Each NC bin takes 187 s: 92 s to refill/replenish, 80 s for the round-trip walk, and 15 s to put the bin away. Folding these in raises the realistic figure to 97.82 s per bin — still 17.38 s better than today. Across the 171 bins replenished line-side each shift, that is roughly 49.5 minutes saved per shift. Every value above is editable.

Extra space required for racks

Space. Rack swapping keeps a second, pre-filled rack ready for each rack in the cycle, and these extra racks are stored in the inventory area. The extra floor space is worked out from two editable figures — the number of racks and the area a single rack occupies. One rack measures 2 ft × 3 ft = 6 sq ft, so at 47 racks the extra space needed is 47 × 6 = 282 sq ft. Change either value and the total recalculates.

Operating assumptions & criteria — how the swap is modelled
A1Full-rack trigger
A rack is exchanged only once every bin in it is depleted, rather than topping up bins piecemeal. This maximises the material moved per swap and minimises the number of line-side trips.
A2NC bins replenished individually
Every bin returned as a non-conformance (NC) is replenished on its own, outside the batch swap, since it falls out of the standard full-rack cycle. This time is costed separately in the model above.
A3A second rack per line-side rack
Each line-side rack has a dedicated, pre-filled duplicate held ready. When a rack runs empty it is swapped for the ready one immediately, and the emptied rack is refilled back in the inventory area as a single batch.
A4Fastener exception — bin-level swap
Because fastener bins carry much higher quantities, the individual bin is transferred from the old rack into the incoming rack rather than swapping the whole rack — avoiding premature refilling of bins that are far from empty.
A5Single-transaction principle
The rules above are chosen specifically to stop any material or bin from being handled and transacted twice. Eliminating that duplicated movement is where the majority of the modelled time saving comes from.

Possibility of improvements
1Labels
Labels are currently typed and printed manually for every item. There is an opportunity to explore whether the new ERP system can generate and print the label — including the quantity — automatically, removing this manual step.
2Stamp, sign & scan
Today the material list is checked, stamped, signed and scanned to re-verify the receipt and archive the documents. The new ERP system may be able to capture this verification and record-keeping digitally, easing the paper handling in this step.
3Misys / Jira
The current process relies on Misys and Jira to confirm orders and to log the associated transactions and communication. The new ERP system may be able to bring these together into a single, faster confirmation and record-keeping step, reducing the time spent moving between separate systems.
